# The Man in the Parking Lot

On the afternoon of August 28, 2003, a delivery driver walked into a bank outside Erie, Pennsylvania, carrying something that looked like a cane. He waited for a teller, then presented a demand for $250,000. Under his shirt, a heavy metal apparatus was fastened around his neck. The note said there was very little time.

The man was Brian Wells, forty-six, a familiar figure to customers of Mama Mia's Pizzeria. Bank robbery did not fit neatly into the life people knew him to have. Neither did the object at his throat. Yet the cane was a disguised firearm, and the threat in the note was sufficiently concrete that the teller handed over the money she could obtain: $8,702, far short of the demand. Wells left with the cash and the weapon. Whatever was happening, it had already departed from the ordinary possibilities of an armed robbery.

## A problem the handcuffs did not solve

Police intercepted Wells nearby. They handcuffed him and placed him on the ground in a parking lot, while officers cleared the surrounding area. Wells said that men had forced the device onto him and ordered him to rob the bank. He was still wearing it. The arrest had secured the money and contained the apparent robber, but it had not resolved the most urgent problem.

For the officers, two interpretations were possible. A man carrying a bomb might be a hostage, or he might be a robber attempting to control the situation through an explosive threat. They had to consider the safety of people around him as well as his own. From a distance, the device did not explain which story was true. Wells could tell them what he believed was happening, but they could not safely test that account by treating the apparatus as harmless.

The bomb squad was called. It did not arrive in time. At 3:18 p.m., the device exploded, killing Wells. The specialists arrived minutes later. Investigators now had an armed bank robbery, a dead delivery driver, and an unusually elaborate object whose maker and purpose were unknown. The apparent perpetrator could no longer answer questions. His death had made the central mystery harder, rather than bringing it to an end.

## The instructions

Wells had not simply been sent to a bank with a demand note. Police found pages of instructions that directed the person described as the bomb hostage through a sequence of tasks. The robbery was only the first stage. Further locations supposedly offered the means to postpone the explosion and eventually free him. Each task was governed by time limits. The instructions discouraged contacting the authorities and represented the route as his only chance of survival.

This was an important change in the meaning of the crime. Someone had designed an experience for Wells to undergo, extending beyond obtaining cash. The papers appeared to promise a way out, but promises written by a criminal are evidence of what the criminal wanted someone to believe, not proof that the promised escape existed.

Investigators tested the route. They could not complete it within the time available. That did not reveal every intention behind every page, but it undermined the premise that prompt obedience would save the wearer. The itinerary might have been a means of controlling him, keeping him moving, or giving the bank robbery the appearance of coercion. It was not a dependable rescue plan.

The bomb itself reinforced the impression of deliberate deception. Its construction included confusing features and apparent distractions. Understanding the device meant separating components that performed a function from components that merely suggested one. Investigators needed to preserve and examine the evidence without accepting the maker's labels at face value. The machine and the instructions were both forms of communication; neither could be assumed to communicate truthfully.

## The delivery at the end of the road

The investigation traced Wells's last delivery. Earlier that afternoon, an order for two pizzas had drawn him to a television transmission tower at the end of a dirt road off Peach Street. The isolated destination supplied a place where someone could confront a driver without an audience of customers or colleagues. It also narrowed the geography of the case.

What happened at that location remained disputed. The official reconstruction eventually held that Wells had agreed to participate in a robbery involving what he believed would be a fake bomb, then discovered that the device was real. His family maintained that he had been forced into the crime without prior knowledge. Those accounts lead to very different judgments of his initial involvement, but both leave room for the same terrible development: by the time he sat in the parking lot, he faced a genuine lethal threat.

It would be tempting to turn the uncertainty into a final twist by choosing whichever version makes the best story. The evidence does not permit that shortcut. The delivery destination is established. The exact sequence by which the collar was attached, and the extent of Wells's advance understanding, are not established with the same confidence. The criminal convictions that followed would not amount to a trial of Wells himself.

For several weeks, the tower and the delivery order seemed to be the best route backward from the bank. Then a nearby resident called the police about something apparently separate: a body in a freezer.

## Another dead man

On September 20, William Rothstein reported that James Roden's body was in a freezer at his property. Roden had been the partner of Marjorie Diehl-Armstrong. Rothstein told investigators that she had killed Roden and that he had helped conceal the body. The discovery connected police to a small circle of people living close to the delivery destination.

Rothstein also produced a note disavowing a connection to Wells's death. That denial was not proof of a connection. Nevertheless, the unsolicited appearance of the name in the account of another death gave investigators a reason to look harder. A claim that two events have nothing to do with each other becomes more interesting when the person making it has not yet satisfactorily explained why they brought up the second event.

Diehl-Armstrong subsequently pleaded guilty but mentally ill to third-degree murder in Roden's death. That resolved responsibility for a different killing, but the freezer discovery had opened a new path into the bank case. Investigators examined relationships among Diehl-Armstrong, Rothstein, and Kenneth Barnes, a man who knew them both. They were no longer searching only for strangers who might have ambushed a pizza driver. They were reconstructing a network whose members knew one another, and whose accounts changed as their own legal situations changed.

Rothstein died of cancer in 2004, before he could be prosecuted over the collar-bomb plot. His death removed another potential defendant and another possible source of testimony. The case would therefore have to be built partly around physical evidence, statements made while he was alive, and testimony from people with strong reasons to minimize their own responsibility.

## What the money was supposed to buy

The explanation that emerged was more convoluted than a simple plan to rob a bank. According to the prosecution, Diehl-Armstrong wanted money to pay Barnes to kill her father, believing that she would benefit from his estate. The bank robbery was intended to finance that killing. The requested $250,000 was connected to the proposed payment, rather than to an arbitrary teller demand.

That account supplied a motive for the amount and linked the robbery to relationships investigators had been uncovering. It also had to be treated critically. Barnes became a cooperating witness; he stood to gain through cooperation. Statements by a participant are valuable, but their value comes from what they explain and how they fit other evidence, not from the participant suddenly becoming disinterested.

The prosecution's case ultimately persuaded a jury. Diehl-Armstrong was convicted in 2010 and sentenced in 2011 to life imprisonment, with an additional consecutive term. Barnes pleaded guilty and received a lengthy prison sentence, later reduced after cooperation. Their cases established criminal responsibility for the plot even though Rothstein could never be tried and Wells's intentions remained contested. Diehl-Armstrong and Barnes both died in prison.

The result was a real resolution, but an uneven one. Investigators could explain the conspiracy and secure convictions. They could not restore the testimony of the dead, and they could not remove every ambiguity from the delivery at the tower. Later documentary accounts offered further claims, including claims about who had selected Wells, but those should not be silently promoted into findings of the jury. The story has enough established horror without improving it through certainty that the record does not supply.

## The promise on the page

The most revealing object may be the itinerary. It presented the wearer with an apparently rational bargain: follow instructions, gather the next item, earn more time. To someone facing a device locked around his neck, that sequence could make obedience feel like a form of agency. There was a next step, so perhaps there was still a solution.

Investigators' reconstruction stripped away that assurance. The route did not fit the allotted time. The same pages that seemed to offer escape could instead keep a man committed to someone else's plan until it was too late. This was why the case required more than identifying the bomb's maker. Its machinery included human expectations: a teller confronted with a deadline, police confronted with an ambiguous threat, and a wearer confronted with a promise that his next act might save him.

Whatever Wells knew before the delivery, the final fact is not disputed. The bomb was real. The promised route to safety was not one investigators could successfully follow. A crime that began with instructions ended with the discovery that the instructions themselves were part of the trap.
