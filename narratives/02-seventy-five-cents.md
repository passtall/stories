# Seventy-Five Cents

In 1986, at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory in California, Clifford Stoll received a task that did not sound like the beginning of an international investigation. Two sets of computer-usage accounts disagreed by seventy-five cents. Someone wanted the discrepancy explained. Stoll, an astronomer who had moved into computer administration, began looking for the missing change.

The laboratory charged for computing time. Its records were supposed to tell compatible stories about who had used the machines and how much that use cost. A small difference could be a bookkeeping mistake, an incomplete entry, or a harmless mismatch between accounting systems. It was worth examining precisely because the systems should agree. Stoll did not initially need a theory about a spy. He needed to find an entry that failed to reconcile.

Instead, the discrepancy led him to a user who did not fit the authorized accounts. Somebody had been inside the computer. The missing money was a trace of an activity that had otherwise gone largely unnoticed.

## The visitor who knew too much

Stoll's later account describes the intrusion as involving elevated privileges obtained through a weakness associated with the Emacs mail-moving utility. The important point was not merely that an unauthorized person could log in. The visitor could interfere with records and operate with powers ordinary users did not have.

That made the accounting mismatch unusually valuable. A person able to alter evidence inside a machine might remove the obvious signs of a visit without understanding every independent system that recorded it. Perfect concealment requires knowing which traces exist. Seventy-five cents suggested that the intruder's knowledge, considerable though it was, was not complete.

The obvious administrative response would have been to close the hole and expel the stranger. Stoll wanted to identify the person and understand what they were doing. Keeping access open was not a decision without risk: the laboratory's machine could serve as a platform for further intrusions. Watching also gave him an opportunity to gather evidence that would disappear if he simply shut the door. The investigation developed out of that tension.

At the time, there was no mature, routine national process for a laboratory administrator to report a network intruder and receive coordinated assistance. Networks connected institutions whose own boundaries were easier to understand than the routes between them. A connection arriving in Berkeley did not necessarily originate with a caller in California. It could have crossed several systems before reaching the laboratory.

## Printing the footsteps

Stoll needed an observation point the intruder could not easily erase. His participant account describes borrowing terminals and printers to monitor incoming connections, then recording the intruder's activity on paper. A log in the compromised computer could be altered. Paper already coming out of a printer was a different problem.

The method sounds cumbersome because it was. Stoll was converting remote commands into physical evidence, line by line, while the visitor continued working. The printouts could reveal both the route into Berkeley and the destinations the intruder attempted to reach from there. They also required someone to notice when activity started and to coordinate with telephone and network operators while the connection was still alive.

The intruder did not behave like someone interested only in free access to a university computer. Through the laboratory, they searched other machines, including military and defense-related systems. Files and terms associated with sensitive projects attracted attention. Some attempted entries failed; others succeeded. Passwords and permissive account settings could make ostensibly important computers surprisingly easy to enter.

The pattern mattered more than any one command. An isolated unauthorized login could be a prank, curiosity, or opportunism. Repeated attention to defense information suggested a purpose. Stoll still could not identify the ultimate customer, but he could show that Berkeley was a stepping stone rather than the final destination.

## A trail with too many owners

Tracing the connection became an exercise in organizational boundaries. A network operator could identify where a session entered its network; a telephone organization could trace a line in its territory; an investigator might need legal authority unavailable to either. None possessed the whole map. Every useful answer generated another institution to contact.

Stoll approached American agencies, including the FBI and organizations concerned with military security and intelligence. His account emphasizes the initial mismatch between the gravity he perceived and the way organizations classified the problem. The immediately visible monetary loss was tiny. Some agencies had limited jurisdiction. Others could not simply take over an investigation on a civilian laboratory's computer.

The bureaucracy was not one undifferentiated obstacle. Eventually, individuals inside it helped. The difficulty was that assistance had to be assembled across institutions designed for other kinds of cases. The network was already international; the ordinary investigative machinery had not yet learned to move at the same speed.

For a time, a connection through a defense contractor in Virginia appeared to supply an American origin. Following the route farther showed why an intermediate computer could mislead. A machine that accepted or relayed a session was not necessarily the machine beside the person at the keyboard. Mistaking the relay for the source would have produced a plausible address and the wrong conclusion.

## The hours that did not fit California

Over months, Stoll kept a logbook. Repeated visits revealed habits as well as commands. The intruder was comfortable with different computer systems and returned at times that invited a geographical explanation. Activity in the middle of the California day could be evening activity elsewhere. This did not identify a person. It supplied a hypothesis that could be checked against the technical traces.

The route eventually led across the Atlantic to West Germany. That was a major advance and a new practical problem. German authorities could trace connections within their telephone system, but they needed enough time while a call was in progress. A visitor who entered, took a small item, and disconnected might escape before all the relevant operators completed their work.

Stoll needed the intruder to linger. He could not reliably command that behavior. He could, however, offer something that appeared to satisfy the interests visible in the logs. The previous searches suggested that material associated with the American Strategic Defense Initiative would be attractive. A file that looked like administrative clutter to a normal user might look like an opportunity to this particular visitor.

## An office that existed only on the computer

Stoll constructed a fictitious project presence on the laboratory's system. It contained documents designed to look like useful material associated with a defense initiative. They were not genuine classified plans. Their function was to hold attention and create further observable behavior.

The trap depended on credibility rather than technical brilliance alone. The material had to be interesting enough to read and large enough to occupy time. It had to resemble the kinds of dull institutional records through which valuable information might be scattered. Real organizations produce paperwork, and a false organization becomes more convincing when it produces paperwork too.

The intruder took the bait. Longer sessions gave the tracing effort a better chance. Stoll's account describes German operators following connections to a home in Hanover. The investigation had finally moved from network abstractions toward an identifiable person: Markus Hess.

Another episode made the fabricated material more revealing. A letter arrived from Hungary seeking information associated with the fictitious project. That inquiry did not, by itself, prove every detail of an intelligence relationship. It did show that information from the bait had travelled beyond the visitor's terminal. Someone was acting on material that had originated inside an invented office. The false documents were becoming a test of the route by which stolen information circulated.

## The customer beyond the keyboard

The broader German investigation established that Hess was part of a group selling computer-derived information to the Soviet KGB. That supplied the missing explanation for the pattern Stoll had watched. The searches were not merely exercises in entering prestigious machines. Access had commercial and intelligence value to people beyond the intruder himself.

Stoll travelled to Germany to testify. Hess and associates were convicted in 1990. The laboratory administrator's observations had become evidence in a case that crossed jurisdictions, technical systems, and the border between ordinary computer misuse and espionage.

The narrative most people know comes from Stoll's book, *The Cuckoo's Egg*. It is an unusually valuable participant account, but it remains a participant account. The improvised monitoring, bureaucratic encounters, and decoy documents should be understood as his description of his work, rather than as scenes reconstructed from a neutral camera. No invented conversation is needed to make the investigation remarkable.

Its strongest feature is the proportional mismatch at the beginning. The seventy-five cents did not represent the real damage or the value of the information being sought. It represented disagreement between records. That disagreement survived because two systems had not been deceived in exactly the same way.

The intruder could reach distant computers and acquire powers that should not have been available. Stoll had something less spectacular: patience, independently preserved observations, and an unwillingness to wave away a number that should have balanced. The visitor had hidden almost everything that made the intrusion seem important. They had left behind just enough to make an astronomer wonder where the money went.
