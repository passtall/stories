# The Illness in the Storeroom

In the summer of 1971, workers at John Hadland Laboratories in Bovingdon, Hertfordshire, began suffering an illness that seemed to move through the workplace. Stomach pain, vomiting, and other symptoms sent people home. A name took hold: the Bovingdon Bug. Naming it made the problem sound like the sort of infection that might arrive, inconvenience a department, and eventually pass.

One of the affected employees was Bob Egle, the storeroom manager. He had survived the evacuation from Dunkirk three decades earlier and was accustomed to work, responsibility, and ordinary difficulties. This illness did not behave like an ordinary short-lived stomach complaint. After a period of improvement, his condition worsened, with neurological symptoms that made the original explanation less adequate. He died on July 7.

## A diagnosis without a source

Egle's death was attributed to Guillain–Barré syndrome, a condition that can cause rapidly developing weakness and paralysis. The diagnosis was not a foolish guess at a trivial symptom. It was an attempt to explain a severe neurological illness through a recognized medical condition.

The difficulty was the wider pattern. Other workers had become ill. Some improved away from the workplace. Further cases included painful sensory disturbances, weakness, and hair loss. Those features did not all fit neatly under the convenient word “bug.” A workplace outbreak might involve infection, an environmental exposure, or several unrelated illnesses being grouped together. At this point, the cause was still a question.

The company made optical equipment, including specialized lenses associated with thallium compounds. That context gave investigators reasons to consider an occupational explanation. Yet the reported account specifies that thallium itself was not stored at the site. A product's connection to a hazardous element does not establish that workers were being exposed to that element in the building.

Alternative ideas included contamination of water or an environmental source nearby. Some of these suggestions were weaker than others. The important point is that management and medical staff were trying to identify a shared hazard without yet knowing whether the shared element was a material, a location, or a person.

## The helpful young employee

Graham Young had joined the company in May as an assistant storekeeper. He was twenty-three and needed employment after a long absence from normal working life. His employers received references connected with rehabilitation, but were not told the full history that would later make his presence so consequential.

Young took an interest in the affected workers. Accounts describe him asking about their conditions and displaying considerable medical knowledge. A colleague who can talk intelligently about illness may initially seem helpful. Expertise becomes suspicious only when it sits beside other information that gives it a different meaning.

Some employees began noticing that Young himself was not suffering from the supposed bug. That observation alone would have been unfair grounds for accusing him. Infectious diseases do not strike every exposed person, and being healthy while others are ill is not evidence of a crime. But coworkers also knew about his unusual interest in poisons.

At a discussion of the illnesses, the firm's medical officer, Iain Anderson, said he had ruled out heavy-metal poisoning. Young challenged that conclusion and argued that the symptoms pointed in that direction. Anderson spoke with him further and encountered a depth of toxicological knowledge that required explanation. The possibility of poisoning was becoming harder to keep separate from the question of this particular employee.

## The second death

During the autumn, additional employees developed serious symptoms. Fred Biggs, who worked part-time at the company, became progressively ill. His condition required hospital care and deteriorated despite efforts to diagnose and treat it. He died on November 19.

By then, the pattern had acquired an urgency that a colloquial label could not contain. Two deaths had occurred in a relatively small workplace, and other people had experienced damaging neurological and systemic illness. The distinction between a temporary virus and an ongoing source was no longer academic. If the source remained present, further people could be affected.

The company contacted police. Investigators compared the timing of the outbreak with Young's arrival and checked his background. What they learned transformed an ambiguous employment history into a crucial lead. Young had not simply been recovering from an unspecified personal breakdown. As a teenager, he had been convicted of poisoning members of his family and a school friend.

He had been detained at Broadmoor, the secure psychiatric hospital, beginning in 1962. He was released in 1971 after clinicians supported the view that he was no longer a danger. The workplace now contained a man with earlier poisoning convictions, a striking interest in the likely cause of the illnesses, and a chronology that fit the beginning of the outbreak.

## The first pattern

The earlier case had also started with illnesses that seemed puzzlingly distributed among people around Young. Members of his household experienced repeated gastrointestinal symptoms. A school friend was affected. Teachers and relatives became concerned about his chemical interests, and investigation eventually established deliberate poisoning.

Young pleaded guilty to three nonfatal poisoning charges. His stepmother had died, and he later claimed responsibility, but her death was not the subject of a murder conviction. That distinction matters. A convicted poisoner's later boast does not automatically convert every earlier suspicious death into an established murder.

Similar caution applies to allegations about deaths or incidents at Broadmoor. Some accounts associated him with events there, but suspicion and colorful retrospective storytelling are not equivalent to proven responsibility. The Bovingdon prosecution did not need those allegations to establish what had happened to Egle and Biggs.

The confirmed earlier convictions gave investigators reason to look closely. They did not replace evidence of the new crimes. A person cannot legitimately be convicted of a second offense merely because they committed a first one. The investigation had to find a connection between Young's current conduct and the illnesses now being examined.

## The diary

Police arrested Young on November 20. A search of his room found poisonous substances and a diary describing his conduct toward coworkers. Initials, recorded observations, and references to their symptoms linked the written material to actual people and actual illnesses.

The diary was important because it made the apparent outbreak intelligible as a sequence of intentional acts. A workplace might accidentally expose people to a substance. Notes discussing particular individuals and the effects on them suggested personal selection and monitoring. What colleagues had experienced as a mysterious illness was something its source had been observing with very different expectations.

Young made admissions to investigators, then later retracted them. At trial, he described the diary as material for a fictional work. That explanation could not be assessed in isolation. Investigators and prosecutors compared it with symptoms, opportunities, chemical findings, and witness accounts. A document is not automatically truthful because it incriminates its writer, but neither does calling it fiction remove the correspondence with events outside the page.

The physical evidence was particularly important. Thallium was found in Biggs's body. Egle had been cremated, which might ordinarily appear to have closed off toxicological investigation. His ashes had not yet been scattered. Analysis found thallium there as well. Material left after cremation still supplied evidence about an exposure that had occurred before death.

## The diagnosis changes meaning

Thallium poisoning can produce a confusing succession of symptoms involving the digestive tract and nervous system, with hair loss among its characteristic signs. Earlier complaints could resemble common illnesses before later features made the pattern more distinctive. The timing and range of effects helped explain why a shared cause had not immediately been recognized.

The account that emerged was that Young had deliberately exposed coworkers through refreshments he handled at work. The narrative does not need the amounts, preparation methods, or particular chemical formulations to explain the investigative breakthrough. What mattered was access to a routine shared activity, the ability to target people within it, and the convergence of records and toxicology afterward.

The colleague apparently interested in the victims' progress was not merely a knowledgeable bystander. His knowledge had an intimate connection to their condition. Questions that initially seemed like concern became evidence of someone tracking the consequences of his own conduct.

In June 1972, Young was convicted of murdering Egle and Biggs, attempting to murder two other employees, and administering poison with intent to injure others. He received a life sentence. He died in custody in 1990. The verdicts gave the outbreak a concrete explanation and identified responsibility for the two deaths.

## The label that concealed a choice

The case prompted scrutiny of release decisions, communication with employers, and controls on poisonous substances. Those questions were legitimate. It would be wrong, however, to turn one offender's conduct into a presumption that all former psychiatric patients are dangerous. The institutional issue was the handling of a specific known history and assessment, not a diagnosis shared by an entire group of people.

The story's investigative reversal lies in the workplace label. A bug arrives from outside human intention. People endure it and wait for it to pass. Here, the distribution of illness reflected decisions made by someone working among the victims.

One of the most revealing pieces of evidence survived an act meant to close a death's physical aftermath. Egle's body had been cremated, but the remaining ashes had not been dispersed. Investigators could still ask them a question. The answer connected the first death to the second and helped turn an unsettling pattern into proof of a crime.
